---
title: "The 100-Hour Week Thing Is a Lie, Actually"
date: 2026-09-10
---

Every few weeks some founder posts about their 100-hour week like it's a personality trait, and everyone in the replies acts impressed instead of concerned. Screenshots of a calendar with no white space. A photo of the office at 2am. Some line about how the people who make it are the ones who outwork everyone else. It gets thousands of likes every time.

I used to feel behind reading those. Like if I wasn't running on four hours of sleep and three energy drinks I wasn't serious enough about the thing I'm building. And when you're building on your own, with no boss and no one to compare against except the internet, that feeling gets loud. You start measuring yourself in hours because hours are the only thing you can count.

Then I actually tried logging my hours for a month, out of curiosity more than discipline. Turns out most days I get maybe four or five hours of work that's actually good. Real, careful, the kind that doesn't need redoing tomorrow. Everything past that is typing that looks like progress and isn't. I'd sit there at hour nine, technically "working," rewriting the same function three times because my brain had already left the building an hour ago.

The 100-hour-week guy isn't lying about the hours, probably. He's lying about the ratio. Nobody posts "I worked 100 hours and maybe 30 of them were useful, the rest was me staring at a screen feeling productive because staring counts as effort if you squint." That doesn't get the same engagement.

And the bad hours aren't free. That's the part that took me longest to see. A tired hour doesn't just produce nothing, it produces bugs, bad decisions and code I have to undo the next morning with a fresh head. So the late hour costs me the late hour plus part of the next good one. On a bad week that adds up to less done than if I'd just gone to sleep.

So I changed how I work, and it's not complicated.

The first good hours go to the hardest thing. Whatever needs actual thinking on PesaScore or Capstone, a design decision, a nasty bug, a data problem, gets the morning or whenever my head is clearest. Not email, not small fixes, not tidying up.

The rest of the day is for the stuff that doesn't need a sharp brain. Replying to people, reviewing, writing things down, small changes I can do half asleep without breaking anything.

When I notice I've read the same line of code three times, I stop. That's the signal. Pushing past it almost never works.

I'm not trying to be the guy who says "just relax bro, work smart not hard," that's its own kind of annoying. Some weeks genuinely need the long hours. Deadlines are real, and PesaScore and Capstone both eat late nights when they need to. Honestly I like the quiet of a late night sometimes. There's a difference between choosing a late night because the problem needs it and grinding every night because you think the hours prove something.

I'm also not pretending I've fixed this. Some weeks I slide right back into it, especially when money is tight and it feels like more hours has to mean more progress. The feeling doesn't go away just because you know it's wrong.

But I'm done pretending the hours themselves are the achievement. The four good hours are the achievement. Everything else is just how you feel about yourself at 1am.
