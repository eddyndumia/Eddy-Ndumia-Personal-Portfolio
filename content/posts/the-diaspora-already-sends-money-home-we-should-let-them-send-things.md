---
title: "The Diaspora Already Sends Money Home. We Should Let Them Send Things."
date: 2026-09-23T00:00:00+03:00
---

Almost every Kenyan family I know has someone abroad. An uncle in Dallas, a cousin nursing in the UK, a sister in Dubai, a friend doing construction in Qatar. And almost every one of those people sends money home. Kenya gets close to five billion dollars a year this way, it's one of the biggest sources of foreign money the country has, bigger than tea, bigger than tourism some years.

Most of that money isn't random. A lot of it is meant for something specific. School fees. A phone for mum because hers has a cracked screen held together with tape. A TV for the house before Christmas. A fridge. A laptop for the kid who just joined campus. The person abroad knows exactly what they want to buy, they just can't walk into a shop in Nairobi and buy it, so they send cash and hope.

And that's where it gets messy. Ask anyone in the diaspora and they'll tell you some version of the same story. They sent money for a phone and the phone never showed up, or a cheaper phone showed up, or the money went to something "more urgent" that nobody mentioned. Sometimes it's family being family. Sometimes it's someone at the other end getting ripped off at a shop on Luthuli Avenue because they didn't know what a real Samsung costs. Either way the person who worked for that money abroad has zero visibility into what happened to it. They just send, and then they send again.

The money part of this has mostly been solved. You can get cash from London into an M-Pesa wallet in a few minutes now, Western Union, WorldRemit, Wise, Sendwave, all of that works. It's still expensive, Sub-Saharan Africa is the most expensive region in the world to send money to, you're usually losing somewhere around eight percent on a small transfer once you add fees and the exchange rate spread. But it works. What hasn't been solved is the trust part. Nobody built the thing where you pay for the actual TV, and the actual TV shows up at your mum's door, and you get a photo of her holding it.

That's the gap I keep coming back to.

The other thing happening at the same time is crypto, specifically stablecoins. I know the word crypto makes people think of meme coins and guys on Twitter selling courses, and fair enough. But underneath all that noise, a very boring and very useful thing happened. USDT and USDC became actual money for a lot of people in Africa. Freelancers here get paid in USDT because it's easier than a foreign bank transfer. Traders importing from China settle in USDT. Kenyans in the Gulf hold USDT on Binance because it's the dollar they can actually get to. Nigeria, Kenya, South Africa keep showing up near the top of the global crypto adoption rankings every year and it's not because everyone is speculating, it's because a dollar that moves in thirty seconds for a few cents is genuinely better than what the banks offer.

So you have two worlds that don't really talk to each other.

Web2 Africa is M-Pesa, WhatsApp, Jumia, the guy with a motorbike who delivers, the shop on Moi Avenue that has been selling phones for fifteen years. It's where real life happens. Your grandmother is in this world and she's not leaving it and she shouldn't have to.

Web3 is wallets, stablecoins, on-chain payments that settle instantly across borders with no bank in the middle. It's where a lot of the diaspora's money and a lot of young Africans' earnings already sit. But almost nothing in the web3 world touches the physical economy here. You can hold a thousand USDT and still not be able to buy a fridge for your parents with it without doing four conversions, two P2P trades and trusting a stranger on Telegram.

I think the next few years of building in Africa are going to be about connecting these two. Not replacing M-Pesa with a blockchain, that's a stupid pitch and nobody here wants it. More like using web3 as plumbing that nobody sees, and keeping web2 as the face everyone already knows. The person paying can use whatever money they have. The person receiving never has to know crypto was involved. They just get the thing.

That's what we're building. It's called Kwetu.

Kwetu in Swahili means "our place," like when you say you're going home, you're going kwetu. It felt right because that's what the whole thing is about. Someone far away buying something for home.

Here's how it works, as simple as I can put it.

You're in Riyadh, or Houston, or Leeds. You open Kwetu in your browser. You pick a phone, a TV, a fridge, whatever. The price is shown in dollars and in shillings, and the price is locked for that order, so no surprise when the rate moves. You pay with USDT or USDC from whatever wallet you use, Binance Pay, MetaMask, Trust Wallet, or with a card or M-Pesa if you don't do crypto at all. You type in who it's for and where they are. That's it on your side.

On our side, we don't hold stock. We work with real shops in Nairobi that already sell this stuff every day. When your order comes in, we convert the stablecoin to shillings through a licensed off-ramp, the shop gets paid in KES on M-Pesa, and a rider takes the item to your family. Your family gets a WhatsApp message saying something is coming for them and who sent it. When it arrives they confirm with a code, and you get a photo and a confirmation that it was delivered to the right person.

A few things I care about getting right, because without them this is just another online shop with a crypto button stuck on it:

The shop only gets fully paid after your family confirms delivery. We hold the money until then. If the item doesn't show up, you get your money back. That one rule is the whole product honestly, it's the thing cash transfers can't give you.

Phones get checked before they leave the shop. The Communications Authority has an IMEI check anyone can run, we run it on every phone and send you the result. Fake and refurbished-sold-as-new phones are a real problem here and the diaspora buyer is the easiest person in the world to sell a fake to, because they're not in the room.

The receipt and warranty are in the name of the person receiving it. So if the TV dies in three months, your mum can walk into the actual shop with the actual receipt. No calling you in Canada to sort it out.

The fee is shown upfront, one number, before you pay. Not hidden in a bad exchange rate. If we can't beat what you'd lose sending cash and hoping, then we shouldn't exist.

Your family never touches crypto. No wallet, no seed phrase, no app to download. They get a WhatsApp message and a delivery. That's the web2 side and it has to feel completely normal.

What this changes, if it works, is small in one way and big in another. Small because it's one mum getting one phone. Big because right now a huge amount of money flows into Kenya every year and a chunk of it leaks, into bad exchange rates, into fake goods, into "I'll explain later." If you give the sender a way to pay for the exact thing and see it arrive, you're not just selling electronics. You're giving people abroad some control over money they worked really hard for, and you're sending real orders to local shops that have never had a diaspora customer before. The shop on Luthuli Avenue gets to sell to Dallas without doing anything different. That's a new market for them that didn't exist yesterday.

And it's not only diaspora. There are a lot of young Kenyans getting paid in USDT for remote work who have to go through a P2P trade just to buy a laptop in their own city. Kwetu works for them too. Pay in the currency you earn in, get the thing locally, done.

I'm not going to pretend this is easy. There's a lot that can go wrong.

Kenya passed the Virtual Asset Service Providers Act in 2025 and I need a proper legal read on where exactly Kwetu sits under it before we take real volume. Banks here can still get twitchy about money that came from crypto, even when it went through a licensed off-ramp. Warranty and returns with a shop you don't own is its own headache. Trust is the whole business, and trust takes time, one bad delivery early on can kill it. And we're doing this on basically no budget, so no warehouse, no ads, no stock. Just partnerships, a website and a lot of WhatsApp.

So the plan is small on purpose. A website, not an app, because wallets work way smoother in a browser and we don't want Apple and Google taking a cut or blocking crypto payments. A short list of products that diaspora people actually buy for home, phones, TVs, a couple of fridges and laptops. Two or three shops we trust. And one goal for the first month, twenty real orders delivered to real families with photos to prove it. If we can't get twenty, we learn why. If we can, we build it properly.

I've been saying for a while that building from Nairobi means half the playbook doesn't apply. This is one of those things where being here is the advantage. You have to have grown up with someone abroad sending money home to understand how much of it goes missing, and you have to be here to know which shop on which street actually sells real phones. Nobody in San Francisco is going to build this, because they don't know the problem exists.

The rails are finally there. Stablecoins that move in seconds, M-Pesa that reaches everyone, WhatsApp that everyone's grandmother already uses. Someone just has to connect them in a way that feels normal on both ends.

We start building tomorrow.
