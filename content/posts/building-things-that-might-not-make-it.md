---
title: "Building Things That Might Not Make It"
date: 2026-09-22
---

Okay so here's the thing nobody puts in the pitch deck.

There's a specific kind of quiet that shows up when you've been building something for months and you genuinely do not know if it survives. Not the dramatic kind, nobody's on the floor about it. The *ordinary* kind. You wake up, check what broke overnight, fix it, move on with your day like a normal person. And underneath that whole normal-person routine there's this hum. Low, constant, the kind you stop noticing until you notice it and then you cannot unnotice it for the rest of the day.

I think about this more than I let on. What does it actually mean to pour a year — two years — into something with no guarantee attached? And I don't mean a *small* guarantee, some soft cushion, I mean none. PesaScore might not work. Capstone might not work. A client might ghost next month and take a third of the pipeline with them. And when people ask "why keep going," the honest answer is embarrassingly unglamorous. It's not passion, it's not some grit-quote-tweet energy. It's closer to: because "not building" has never once felt like the safer option to me, even though on paper it obviously, mathematically, is.

## a confession

Nobody tells you that courage doesn't feel like courage from the inside. From the inside it feels like you didn't have a better idea, so you kept doing the one you had, and you did it as well as you could on the good days and badly on the bad ones and called both of those "shipping." Discipline isn't a personality trait some people are born with and some aren't. It's just whatever's left standing after the motivation clocks out and you're still there, keyboard warm, cursor blinking at you like it's waiting for an explanation you don't have.

I'm not going to pretend there's a bow to tie on this. I'm not out of the woods. I genuinely don't know if I will be. But there's something almost *calming* about saying that plainly instead of performing a confidence I haven't earned yet. The work doesn't care either way. The commit still has to go in. The bug still needs fixing regardless of how existentially I'm spiraling about it at 11pm.

Turns out existential dread does not exempt you from writing clean code. If anything it makes you *more* careful, because you already know how little margin you have for a bug you'll be too tired to catch.

So I build anyway. Slowly, honestly, one working feature at a time, without pretending I know how the story ends — because I don't, and at this point pretending would just be one more thing I'd have to debug later.
