---
title: "Building Things That Might Not Make It"
date: 2026-09-22
---

I've been building PesaScore and Capstone for months now and I still don't know if either one makes it. Not in some dramatic way, nobody's crying about it. Just, some mornings I check what broke overnight, fix it, and get on with the day like normal. But it's there under everything. There's no guarantee attached to any of this.

Most of what you read about startups is written after the fact. The founder who made it looks back and everything sounds planned. The pivot was genius, the hard year was character building, the early doubters were wrong. You almost never read the version written in the middle, when nobody knows how it ends, including the person writing. This is that version.

People ask why I keep going and honestly there's no clean answer for it. It's not passion, it's not some grit speech I tell myself. It's just that not building never once felt like the safer option to me, even though on paper it clearly is. A job would pay rent on time. Building pays nothing until it suddenly does, if it ever does.

Nobody tells you this doesn't feel brave from the inside. It just feels like you didn't have a better idea, so you kept doing the one you had. Some days you do it well, some days you don't, and you call both of those shipping. That's discipline, really. Not a personality trait. Just whatever's left after the motivation runs out and you're still at the keyboard.

Here's what a normal week actually looks like. Fix what broke. Talk to someone who might use the thing, and listen more than I talk. Build the next small piece. Write down what I learned. Check the money. Repeat. None of it is exciting. Most of it wouldn't make a good tweet.

A few things that keep me going when the big question has no answer:

I make the steps small. I can't know if PesaScore works as a business this year. I can know if the scoring works on the next hundred statements. Small questions have answers, and answers feel better than hope.

I keep a record. Every feature that ships, every bug that got fixed, every conversation with a real user. On bad days I read it back, not to feel good, just to see the thing actually moved.

I'm honest about the money. Runway is a number, not a feeling. Knowing exactly how many months are left is scary, but not knowing is worse.

I don't build alone. Having people around who are in it with you changes how the bad weeks feel, even if it doesn't change the numbers.

I'm not out of the woods. I don't know if I will be. Things could go wrong in a hundred ways, the market might not care, the money might run out first, someone bigger might do it faster. Saying that plainly feels better than pretending I'm sure of something I'm not. The work doesn't care either way. The bug still needs fixing whether I'm spiraling about it or not.

And if it doesn't make it, it won't have been wasted. I know more about data, lending, agents and building for this market than I did a year ago, and that doesn't go away. The next thing starts from here, not from zero.

So I keep building. Slowly, one working feature at a time, without pretending I know how it ends.
