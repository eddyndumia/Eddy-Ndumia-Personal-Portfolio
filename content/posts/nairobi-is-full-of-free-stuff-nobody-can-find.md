---
title: "Nairobi Is Full of Free Stuff. Nobody Can Find It."
date: 2026-09-23T12:00:00+03:00
---

Every time someone moves house in Nairobi the same thing happens. The lorry is booked for Saturday, the new place is smaller, and there's a sofa, a microwave, a baby cot and three boxes of books that are not coming along. Nobody wants to sell them. Selling means photos, haggling, strangers asking "last price" at 11pm, people who say they're coming and then don't. So the stuff goes to the watchman, or the mama fua, or it sits outside the gate until it rains, or it ends up at Dandora with everything else.

It's not only movers. Expats finishing a contract leave whole houses behind. Students clear out hostels at the end of the year. Offices upgrade and don't know what to do with twenty working chairs. Families have a spare TV in the store since they bought the bigger one. Almost every house I know has at least one thing that works fine and that nobody uses.

And on the other side of the same city there's someone who needs exactly that thing. A student furnishing a bedsitter in Roysambu with nothing. A young couple with a baby on the way and no budget for a cot. A guy starting a small kibanda who needs a fridge. Somebody who would genuinely be happy with a used microwave that someone in Kilimani is about to throw away.

The two people are sometimes three kilometres apart. They just never meet.

The tools we have don't really fix this. Jiji and Facebook Marketplace are built for selling, so everything on them has a price and a negotiation attached. WhatsApp groups work for a bit, then turn into noise and forwarded messages. Estate groups are small and you only see what your immediate neighbours post. There's no place where you can open your phone and see "here's what people near you are giving away right now." That's the gap.

There's also a trust problem nobody talks about. If something is free, people claim it and don't show up. Or they show up and it's not what they expected. Or the giver changes their mind. Free sounds simple but free is actually where the most flakiness lives, because nobody has anything to lose. Anyone who has tried to give something away in a WhatsApp group knows this. Ten people say "me!", one person comes, three days later.

So we built Chukua.

Chukua in Swahili means "take." It's the word you say when you hand someone something. Here, chukua. It felt right because that's the whole app. Someone has something they don't need, someone else takes it.

Here's how it works, as simple as I can put it.

If you're giving, you open the app, take a few photos, pick a category and a condition, and say roughly where you are and when it has to be gone by. That's about thirty seconds. You don't set a price because there isn't one. Your phone number is never shown on the listing.

If you're taking, you open the app and see what's near you, sorted by distance. The default is 5 km, you can widen it to 20 km if you're willing to travel. Every item shows how far it is and what it's worth, because the app runs an AI value estimate on the photos and gives you a range, like KES 4,000 to 6,000 for a woofer. When you find something you want, you claim it. The item is held for you, the exact pickup spot is unlocked, and you arrange your own transport, a boda, a tuktuk, a friend with a pickup, whatever works.

That's the whole flow. See it, claim it, pick it up.

A few things I care about getting right, because without them this is just another WhatsApp group with a nicer screen:

Claiming costs a little money. Right now that's KES 50 paid through M-Pesa. I went back and forth on this a lot, because the whole point is that the stuff is free. But a small fee is the difference between "me!" and actually showing up. If you've paid fifty bob you come. The giver's time matters too.

Everyone has a verified phone number. You can't list or claim without one. No anonymous accounts, no fake profiles claiming ten items to resell.

There are limits built in. You can only have three open claims at a time, and a giver can post up to twenty listings a day. If you claim and don't show up, you get a strike, and enough strikes means you can't claim for a while. None of this is exciting but it's what keeps the thing fair for the people using it honestly.

The value estimate is a range, not a price. It's there so you know if a trip across town is worth it, and so the giver can see what they're actually giving away. Some people find that last part weirdly satisfying, seeing that they gave away thirty thousand shillings worth of stuff this year.

Listings expire. If something isn't picked up by the date the giver set, it disappears, so the feed is always stuff that's actually available and not a museum of old posts.

It works in English and Swahili. A lot of the people who need free stuff most are not the people who want to read an app in English.

What this changes, if it works, is small and also not small. Small because it's one cot going from one house to another. Not small because it happens thousands of times across a city of five million people, and every one of those is something that didn't get thrown away and something someone didn't have to buy. The giver clears space without the headache of selling. The taker gets something useful for the price of a boda ride. Dandora gets a little less.

It also turns giving into something you can see. Right now if you give your old stuff to the watchman you have no idea if it helped anyone. On Chukua you see that it got picked up and what it was worth. That sounds small but I think it's what makes people do it again.

I'm not going to pretend this is easy.

The hardest part of any marketplace is the start. Nobody opens an app with nothing on it, and nobody lists on an app nobody opens. So we're starting small on purpose, a few neighbourhoods in Nairobi first, Kilimani, Westlands, Kileleshwa, Lavington, South B and C, Roysambu, Kasarani. Places where people move a lot and where there's a mix of people with too much and people with not enough.

Safety is real. You're meeting strangers to pick up stuff. You can choose to meet in public instead of at home, the app keeps the exact location hidden until someone has claimed and paid, and has a report button on every listing and user. It's not perfect and I don't think anything in this space ever is.

Resellers will come. Someone will try to claim everything good and flip it on Jiji. The claim limits help, but I'm sure people will find ways around them and we'll have to keep adjusting.

And we're doing this on basically no budget. No marketing money, no big team. SMS for login codes costs money we haven't spent yet, and M-Pesa payments are still being wired properly. Everything is being built so it can run for close to nothing until it earns its own keep.

So the plan is simple. Android first, because that's what almost everyone here has. A small area of Nairobi. And one real goal for the first month, real items going from real people who didn't need them to real people who did, with no money changing hands except the claim fee. If people show up for their claims, it works. If they don't, we learn why and fix it.

I keep coming back to the same idea with everything I'm building right now. The stuff that actually matters here is usually boring. Not a new social network or some giant platform, just connecting two people in the same city who would help each other if they knew the other one existed. Nairobi already has the people with extra and the people with nothing. It already has M-Pesa, and bodas that can move a sofa across town for a few hundred bob. Someone just has to put them on the same map.

That's Chukua. Free stuff near you.
