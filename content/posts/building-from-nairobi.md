---
title: "Building From Nairobi While Everyone Else Talks About San Francisco"
date: 2026-09-15
---

Every founder story I read growing up had the same backdrop. Some garage in California, some dorm room at a school everyone's heard of, some accelerator with a demo day that gets written about the next morning. The problems were always American problems too. Splitting a restaurant bill. Finding a parking spot. Booking a dog walker. I read all of it and liked a lot of it, but none of it looked like where I was sitting.

Meanwhile I'm building PesaScore off M-Pesa statements. A payment rail that most of the people writing those stories have literally never used, for a market they've never priced anything for.

Here's the thing most startup content skips. In Kenya a huge number of people have never had a credit bureau file worth anything. They pay rent through M-Pesa. They buy stock for their kiosk through M-Pesa. They send money to their mum, get paid by their customers, pay school fees, all of it through M-Pesa. Their whole financial life is right there in a statement. But when they walk into a bank or a SACCO asking for a loan, the lender asks for payslips and a credit history they don't have, and the conversation ends.

So "alternative data" here isn't a buzzword. It's the only data that exists for a lot of people. The alternative data IS the data. That's not a scrappy underdog narrative, it's just what building for this market actually requires. And most of the frameworks and blog posts about credit scoring assume a bureau file exists in the first place, so half of what you read doesn't apply before you've even started.

That's what PesaScore does, as simple as I can put it. You take the statement someone already has, you read the patterns in it, how regular the money coming in is, how they handle a bad month, whether bills get paid on time, and you turn that into a score a lender can actually use. No new habits for the borrower. They just share what they already have.

A few things I've learned from doing this from here and not from somewhere else:

Nobody's watching this build the way people watch a YC demo day. There's no hype cycle checking in on whether a SACCO in Nairobi actually wants a better way to score a loan applicant. That's honestly fine, mostly. It means nobody's going to overreact to a slow week. But it also means there's no outside pressure forcing the pace either. You have to actually want it enough to keep going without an audience.

The playbook doesn't transfer. Growth tactics, pricing advice, even how people think about trust, a lot of it assumes a customer with a card, a bank account and an email they check. Here the customer might have none of those and still run a business that moves more money in a month than some salaried people do. You find out what doesn't apply by hitting the wall, not by reading about it first.

Being close to the problem is the advantage. I don't need a survey to understand how a mama mboga's M-Pesa statement looks, or why a SACCO officer doesn't trust a number from a black box. I've seen it. Someone in San Francisco would need months of research to learn what's normal here, and they'd still miss things.

I'm not going to pretend it's all upside. Capital is thinner here. Fewer people around you have built this kind of thing before, so a lot of questions you just answer yourself. Regulation around data and lending is real and you have to take it seriously from day one. And when you explain what you're doing to people outside the space, half of them nod and the other half ask when you'll get a real job.

I'm not saying any of this to complain. I actually think it's the more interesting problem. The tools that will matter for the next few years in Africa are going to be built by people who understand M-Pesa statements, SACCO meetings and kiosk economics from the inside. That's not a disadvantage to hide. That's the whole edge.

So I keep building from here. Half the playbook doesn't apply, and that's fine. We're writing the other half.
