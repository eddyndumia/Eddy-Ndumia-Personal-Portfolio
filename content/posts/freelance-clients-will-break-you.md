---
title: "Freelance Clients Will Break You If You Let Them"
date: 2026-09-24
---

Been freelancing since 2020 and nobody warns you about the specific kind of tired that comes from a client who "just has one small thing" for the fourth time this week. It's never small. It is never, ever small.

A lot of developers here freelance. For some it's the whole income, for some it's what pays rent while they build their own thing on the side. It's the most common path I know into real work, because a portfolio of shipped client projects gets you further than a certificate. But nobody teaches the client side of it. You learn the code from tutorials. You learn the clients by getting burned.

A few patterns I've noticed, no particular order.

The client who goes quiet for three weeks and then messages "hey are we still on track" like they didn't ghost the whole thing. The client who approved the design, then two days before launch wants to "explore a different direction," which is code for starting over. The client who wants a quote for "a simple app like Uber." The client who pays on time every single month and somehow that alone makes every other headache worth it, which tells you how low the bar actually is.

I used to take every scope-creep request personally, like it meant I did something wrong upfront. Now I just quote it separately and move on. Not because I stopped caring, I just ran out of energy to be offended by normal business behavior. People don't know what they want until they see what they didn't want. That's not malice, it's just how it goes.

The thing that actually changed how I work wasn't a contract clause or a better onboarding form, though those help. It was accepting that a chunk of freelance work is just managing someone else's uncertainty about their own project, and charging accordingly. Once I stopped expecting clients to know exactly what they wanted, the job got a lot less personally exhausting and a lot more just... a job.

Here's how I work now, as simple as I can put it.

Everything gets written down before I start. What's included, what isn't, when it's due, what it costs. Not a long legal document, just a clear list both of us have agreed to in writing. When the "one small thing" shows up, I point at the list.

Money comes before work. A deposit before I start, and the rest tied to things we can both see, like a working demo. It's not about trust, it's that unpaid work is the fastest way to start resenting a client.

Changes get their own quote. Not a fight, not a guilt trip. Just "sure, that's outside what we agreed, here's what it costs and how long it adds."

Show things early. The sooner a client sees something real, the sooner they find out what they actually wanted. It's much cheaper to change direction at week one than at launch.

Protect the hours. Clients get fast replies during working hours. They don't get my 11pm unless something is actually on fire.

I'm not going to pretend I always hold these lines. When money is tight you say yes to things you shouldn't, you start before the deposit clears, you let the scope slide because losing the client feels worse. Freelancing when rent is due is not the same as freelancing with savings. I still get it wrong sometimes.

But the good clients stay, and the good ones are worth it. They're the ones who respect the list, pay on time, and come back with the next project. After a while you start to recognize them in the first call.

Still doing it though. Six years in. Guess it's not that bad.
